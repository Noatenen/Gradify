PRODUCT DECISION · IA, NOT UI

Should Motiva have two workspaces instead of one?

No visual design here — this is a navigation and architecture question. Skipping straight to a better-organized Tasks page would have papered over a real mismatch: My Tasks and Submissions aren't two flavors of the same list, they're two different jobs.

UX ANALYSIS

Why the combined page was the actual problem

Different goals. Opening "My Tasks" means "what do I execute right now" — a personal/team productivity mindset. Opening "Submissions" means "where does my formal deliverable stand with my supervisor" — an approval/status mindset, closer in spirit to Requests than to Tasks. Putting both under one heading asked the student to hold both mindsets at once, which is exactly the "constant context switching" you flagged.

Different lifecycles. A team task is binary-ish (not started → in progress → done). A submission has a real multi-party lifecycle with a gatekeeper (draft → ready → submitted → under review → revision requested → approved). Compressing the second into the first's visual language (which the previous round already worked around with a stage-strip) was a symptom of the deeper issue: they don't belong in the same list, styled or not.

Different stakes and cadence. Tasks are frequent and low-stakes to check; submissions are infrequent and high-stakes (they involve a supervisor's judgment). A student anxious about a Final Report shouldn't have to scroll past "design the dashboard" to find it, and vice versa — a five-task afternoon shouldn't be interrupted by a submission-review banner that isn't actionable yet.

Verdict: the combined single-page approach should be abandoned as the primary structure. It optimized for "everything in one place" over "each thing in the right place" — the wrong trade for two genuinely different jobs.

ARCHITECTURE OPTIONS

ACombined single page

Current direction — one "Tasks" page, two zones.

Cognitive load: highest — every visit mixes two mindsets.

Navigation: zero extra clicks, one destination.

Recommendation: abandon as the primary structure.

BSegmented tabs, one page

One nav entry, a switcher fully replaces the content per tab.

Cognitive load: low while on a tab — but the two still feel like sub-modes of one thing.

Navigation: one click to switch; easy to forget the other tab exists.

Recommendation: viable fallback, not the strongest option.

CTwo peer workspaces

"המשימות שלי" and "הגשות" as separate sidebar destinations.

Cognitive load: lowest — each destination is one mindset, fully.

Navigation: one extra sidebar item (6→7), but each entry is unambiguous.

Recommended.

DMilestone-first nesting

No task/submission split at the top; both live inside each milestone.

Cognitive load: medium — clear per-milestone, but mixed again within each group.

Navigation: no new nav item, but no "all submissions at a glance" view either.

Recommendation: better as a milestone detail view than as the primary Tasks/Submissions structure.

RECOMMENDATION

Option C — split into two peer workspaces: "המשימות שלי" (execution — team/personal tasks) and a new "הגשות" destination (formal submission lifecycle), each a full page, each simple because it only holds one kind of work. This matches how the student actually thinks about the two ("chores I do" vs. "what my supervisor thinks of my deliverable") better than a shared tab or a shared page ever could.

Where the combined glance still lives: the Dashboard already surfaces both types together in "Requires my attention" and "Recent updates" — that's the right place for a cross-cutting summary. Splitting Tasks and Submissions doesn't remove the unified view, it just moves it to the one page whose job is already "where do I stand, overall."

Trade-off, stated plainly: the sidebar grows from 6 to 7 items. That's an acceptable cost for two workspaces that are each simpler and unambiguous, and it mirrors an intentional distinction already in the product: Submissions sits closer in spirit to Requests (both are supervisor-facing, lifecycle-driven) than to Tasks (self-directed execution) — placing it as its own nav entry, ideally adjacent to Requests, reflects that. Option B (tabs) is the fallback if a new sidebar entry turns out to be politically or technically costly, but it only partially solves the problem — a tab boundary still implies "these are variants of one thing," which is precisely the framing we're trying to correct.

SELF-CORRECTION

Every option above was still "which task-manager page." That's the actual mistake.

Options A–D all treated the project as a subtitle under a task list. Even the recommended Option C turns "the project" into one line of context at the top of two otherwise independent pages — exactly what loses the thread. The real fix isn't where Tasks and Submissions live; it's that the project itself needs to be permanent chrome, not a per-page header that scrolls away. Tasks and Submissions should feel like two things you can look at inside the project — never two places you navigate away to.

The Project Workspace Shell

SCROLLED TO TOP

SCROLLED DOWN — PROJECT RAIL STAYS PUT

The project name, overall progress, and milestone timeline become a sticky rail — present above whatever you're looking at, at every scroll position, on every lens. Directly beneath it, an in-place lens switch (Tasks / Submissions) changes only the content area; the rail itself never resets, never disappears, never has to be "remembered" from a different page.

ProjectWorkspace
├─ Sidebar (global: Dashboard · Notifications · Team News · Requests · Knowledge)
└─ Project frame (one screen, one URL)
    ├─ ProjectRail — sticky: name, overall progress, milestone timeline  (never scrolls away)
    ├─ LensSwitch — "משימות" / "הגשות", inline, directly under the rail
    └─ LensContent — swaps in place: Task rows, or Submission stage-strips

Why this beats Option C (two separate destinations): C fixed the mixed-mindset problem but reintroduced the exact loss-of-context the brief is now naming — leaving Tasks means leaving the project. Here, switching lenses is a change of attention, not a change of place; the project is the constant, tasks and submissions are just two ways of looking at it.

Why this beats Option B (plain tabs): a tab bar alone still implies "two variants of a list." A persistent, sticky project rail above the switch changes what the tabs mean — they're not modes of a task manager, they're lenses on a project that's always in frame.
